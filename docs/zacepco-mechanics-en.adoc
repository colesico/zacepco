== About the document

The document describes the mechanics of a detective game and sets the general rules by which it must be built.  
For the integrity of the game, an integral supplement to this mechanic is the *crime scenarios*, which describe the plot component.  
The mechanic itself is essentially a *framework* of a deductive detective game with elements of randomness.

== Crime scenario

Within the mechanics, a scenario describes a crime that has already occurred.  
During the game, the players (detectives) must solve this crime.  
The scenario is oriented toward different degrees of difficulty in solving the crime, describing, for example, one crime or several at once, with the participation of one or several criminals, with or without accomplices, with different numbers of suspects, crime duration, size of the “theater of action”, etc.

Any scenario contains:

* Instructions for preparation before the investigation begins.
* Information for the course of the investigation.
* An “Answers” section that can be viewed at the end of the game.

Ready-made scenarios come with the game or are generated by a special program.

The game takes place according to the scenario chosen by the players and is divided into two *phases*.

In the first phase, the players, according to the scenario, perform preparatory actions to begin the investigation: assembling the game board, etc.

The second phase is the investigation, a cooperative process of solving the crime by the detectives.

At the end of the game, the investigation's conclusions are compared with the scenario's “Answers” section.

== Master Program

To moderate the game process, a computer program — the *“Master Program”* — is used; it is configured by the scenario for a specific game.  
The program also automates all calculations related to the game process and controls the game mechanics.

== Game entities and relations

The scenario operates with the following types of *game entities*:

* *Personage (P)* — an actor in the crime;
* *Location (L)* — part of the crime scene;
* *Clue (C)* — part of information about the crime;
* *Time (T)* — a moment in time during the crime.

The game entities themselves (except time) are represented by entity cards.  
Each such card has its own format depending on the entity type.  
An entity is identified by a unique identifier consisting of a letter — the entity type — and a unique number. For example P2, L5, C55.

Time has no cards of its own, only an ordinal number (T1, T2, …) and a time binding.

Each game entity may be related by the scenario to any other entities.  
From the point of view of a relation, when one entity references another, the first is called the *key entity*, and the second is called the *target entity*.

The scenario defines a set of game entities and their relations, which essentially describes the crime (determines the crime model).

Some of these entities and relations may be unrelated to the crime and serve to complicate the investigation process (false clues).

Before the investigation begins, detectives know a certain small part of the entities and their relations.  
During the investigation, detectives gradually discover the remaining entities and relations.

In the game, entities are represented by cards. Some entity cards used in the scenario are already prepared — *“reference entities”* — while some require production — *“custom entities”*. They are provided as layouts in the scenario and must be printed independently before the game. Reference entities can be reused in different scenarios; if necessary, the scenario defines custom entities.

== Discovery of entities and relations

The game uses a mechanic of *staged* discovery by detectives of entity cards and detection of relations between them.

Based on some already discovered key entities, detectives can learn their relations with other target entities, both discovered and not yet discovered.  
Then they can choose other key entities and again determine the next target entities and relations, and so on.

Depending on the specific game mechanic, entity relations are described by the scenario in the form of *relation tables* of a certain format.

When obtaining information from relation tables, the *Master Program* is used so as not to reveal all information about the crime to the detectives at once.

== Element of randomness

D12 die.

== Resource, points

In the game, each detective action costs a certain *resource*.  
If the action is successful, the player is awarded *points*.  
The scenario determines the total *budget* of resource for the investigation.  
When the budget is exhausted, the investigation stops, and the detectives must draw conclusions from the available data and compare the results with the answers.

The individual number of earned points determines the best detective in the investigation, provided that the detectives have solved the crime. If the detectives could not solve the crime, the earned points are not taken into account.

== Game time

The crime scenario covers a time interval divided into *ticks* (`T1, T2, … Tn`) — equal or unequal time intervals.

Each tick has a time label (date-time), for example `T4 = 21:30`. The label sets the *right boundary* of the tick. Events of `T4` occur after the label of the previous tick and no later than `21:30`. The scenario contains a publicly available “Timeline” table in which each tick is assigned a time label.
The difference between neighboring labels must be plausible: within a tick, a personage must have time to perform the actions implied by the scenario.

All significant crime events occur in specific ticks; clues are tied to tick time labels, not to time values inside the tick itself. Even if, for example, for T3 = 21:00, T4 = 21:30, a clue states the time 21:20, it is considered that the event occurred in tick T4, and it does not matter exactly when within the tick. The exact order of events within a tick is not defined and is not used for deduction, i.e., all events within a tick are consistent from the point of view of clues.

The more ticks, the more difficult it is to solve the crime.



Relation tables, focuses, and logs use only the tick number.


== Crime scene, locations, relations

In the scenario, the crime scene is defined as a graph.  
During the investigation preparation phase, the players use this graph to assemble the crime scene — the *scene* — from individual *locations* (graph nodes). The scene is a grid of a certain size (the grid size is specified by the scenario). A location is placed in any cell of the scene grid. Some cells of the scene may be left empty.

Each location is represented by a square location card (reference or custom). The location card contains the following information:

* ID;
* Name;
* Description;
* Location image.

Two adjacent locations may be connected by *ways* that determine whether a personage can move between them.  
If two non-adjacent locations need to be connected, way cards with different configurations (straight, turn) are used; they are laid out one after another in empty scene cells, connecting the required locations.  
A way always connects only two locations. Two locations can be connected by only one way.

In the scenario, for movement along a way, some restrictions or characteristics of the way can be specified in the form of a *description*. For example: a river 10 meters wide. If there is no way description, it is considered that for the investigation only the very fact of the possibility of moving from one location to another is important.

Nothing ever happens to personages on the way itself;  
if some action of a personage on the way needs to be modeled, the way is replaced by a full-fledged location.

The scenario may also assume the existence of *“hidden” ways* between locations, which are not marked during the investigation preparation phase.  
Detectives learn about the existence of such ways during the investigation.  
When a clue indicating the existence of a hidden way (*hidden-way clue*) is discovered in some location, the ID of the target location to which the way leads is determined.  
A way card is placed on the scene, connecting the location in which the hidden-way clue was discovered with the target location.

A hidden way may lead to a *hidden location*.  
This is a location that detectives do not know about before the investigation begins.  
A hidden location is one of the *reference* locations not yet placed on the scene.  
A discovered hidden location is placed on the scene in the cell specified in the scenario.  
The location is connected by a way to the location where the hidden-way clue was discovered.  
After discovery, the location becomes available for investigative actions.

Information about all hidden ways and locations is included in the scenario's *answers*.

When developing a scenario, locations and way descriptions must be defined in such a way that their meaning in the investigation is taken into account.  
For example, there may be no light in a cave, a personage may enter the basement only with a key, a poisonous plant may grow in a greenhouse, the bar is open from the 3rd through the 6th tick, etc.  
The semantic load and descriptions of locations and ways must help detectives in the investigation.

For each location, the scenario describes the event log (log) of that location for each time tick.  
A log event contains information about which personages had this location as a target in the tick, and which clues appeared in the location as a result of a personage's actions.

The event log is used at the end of the game when checking the investigation's conclusions and is unavailable to players during the investigation.

== Personages

An actor in the crime is a *personage*.  
Each personage is represented by a (reference or custom) personage card.  
The personage card contains the following information:

* ID;
* Name;
* Dossier (description);
* Personage image.

The set of personages in the game is determined by the scenario.  
The scenario defines explicit personages — they are known before the investigation begins.  
Their cards are placed next to the game board.

The scenario may also imply the presence of *hidden personages*.  
A hidden personage is one whose card has not yet been placed face up on the table from the personage deck.  
A hidden personage is one of the *reference* personages.

The presence of a hidden personage can be determined during the investigation logically, on the basis of discovered clues, or by discovering a clue with a direct reference to this hidden personage (similar to clues indicating a hidden way).  
When a clue with a reference to a hidden personage is discovered, that personage's card is placed face up from the personage deck next to the game board with the cards of the other personages.  
After that, the personage becomes available for investigative actions.

In the scenario, in the “Answers” section, all hidden personages are listed.

Personages in the scenario must be described in such a way that the description helps detectives in the investigation.

== Personage movement and actions

The mechanics separates the concepts of *movement* and *action*.
For each personage during the crime, *in each time tick* Ti, there is only one of the following alternatives:

* Stay in the current location and do nothing.
* Stay in the current location and then perform *one* *action*.
* Move to an adjacent location and do nothing.
* Move to an adjacent location and then perform one *action*.

Movement occurs to an adjacent location if they are connected by a *way*.  
On the scenario side, there may be restrictions on the way, for example, a door that must be opened with a key.  
In the general case, a personage may visit the same location several times in different ticks.  
For easier game variants, a personage may visit a location only once.

The personage's movement track (sequence of locations) is implied by the scenario but is not explicitly revealed to the players.  
Players can determine this track during the investigation.

Personage tracks are revealed by the scenario as “answers”: for each personage, their movements are indicated as a sequence of location IDs for each tick (in the personage log).  
This information can be viewed at the end of the game.

During the investigation phase, to visualize the personage's movement way (*track*) across the game board, as one of the possible approaches, tokens of the personage's color are placed in locations.  
Each token bears the number of the tick in which the personage was in the location where the token lies.  
Such tokens are called personage track tokens. As another example, in the detective's notebook it is noted which locations the personage presumably visited.

When in the *target* location, a personage may perform any *one* action.  
The mechanic does not provide for the action itself as an entity, but the result of this action becomes a *clue* that is related either to the location, to the personage, or to expertise.
 
   For example, a personage saw another personage. The first personage gains a clue in the form of testimony, which can be revealed during interrogation.

For each personage, the scenario describes the event log (log) of that personage for each time tick.  
A log event contains information about which target location the personage was in and which clue was generated by them (for example, left a trace in the location or a trace remained on the personage).

The event log is used at the end of the game when checking the investigation's conclusions and is unavailable to players during the investigation.

== Clues

A *clue* is a piece of information about the committed crime that detectives discover sequentially during the investigation.  
A clue may be a physical object, an event, some information, or a fact.

Examples of clues: testimony, motive, note, cigarette butt, habit, a personage's addiction to smoking, the fact that an object is absent from a location, an expert report.

The crime scenario determines the set of clues participating in the game.  
A clue is represented by a (reference or custom) clue card with the following printed on it:

* ID;
* Name;
* Description (detailed);
* Clue image.

Some clues may be unrelated to the crime and serve to make the game more difficult (false clues). The more such clues, the more difficult the game.

Initially, clues are hidden from detectives.  
During the investigation, detectives gradually “discover” clues — place clue cards face up.

If a discovered clue contains references to other entities not yet discovered, those entities are also discovered (recursively). The scenario must not define deep recursion of discoverable entities.

Examples of clues:

* Clue C1: “Testimony. Saw personage P1 (Maid) during tick 4 in location L2 (Dining Room)”.
* Clue C2: “In this location, a hidden passage to location L4 (Basement) was found”.
* Clue C6: “In this location, C2 (Candlestick) is missing, but it was originally here”.
* Clue C12: “Broken clock. The hand points to tick T8”.
* Clue C13: “In this location, personage P1 (Gardener) was noticed in tick T3 with C6 (Knife) in hand”.

== Variable values

For universalization of printed entity cards, placeholders are indicated in them instead of concrete values/text, and their values are determined in the scenario.

An example of using placeholders for a clue:

====
C1: Saw personage $P during tick $T in location $L
====

The values of placeholders P, T, L are taken from the scenario from the placeholder table.

====
L2: Location $N. Description: $D
====

The name N and description D are taken from the scenario.

== Reuse of entities, reference and custom entities

A scenario may use some entities from other (*reference*) scenarios.
To do this, the scenario lists these entities in the form in which they are defined in the reference scenario (as ordinary scenario entities).  
Such entities are also called *reference entities*.

All scenario entities that are not reference entities are considered *custom entities* (local scenario entities) and are printed before the game begins.
Reference entity cards are taken from the reference scenarios (if they were printed earlier), or are also printed.

For convenience, reference entities have a note indicating from which scenario they are borrowed.

== Location search

During the investigation, detectives may *“search”* any discovered location (including a previously hidden one) to try to discover clues related to that location.  
As a result of the search, a clue may be “found” (discovered) with some probability.

The scenario defines the *“Location search”* relation table, which determines the relation between locations and clues.  
During a search, the mechanic of probabilistic clue discovery is used together with the *“Search focus”* mechanic.  
The focus makes it more likely to discover the clue targeted by the search focus and to get more points.  
However, the strategic goal of the focus is not so much to “obtain” a clue as to *formulate a hypothesis for testing*.  
The focus consists of two parts: the table focus (reference) and the detective focus.

A location search costs a certain resource. Points may be awarded to the detective for the search.

Search procedure:

1. The detective declares a search in a specific location, for example L1.
2. Specifies the detective focus — a set of IDs of any *discovered* entities or any types, for example: T4, L?, C?.
3. Rolls a D12 die to model the probabilistic nature of clue discovery.
4. Using the “Location search” table, the search result is determined.  
   Access to the search table is provided through the Master Program.

=== “Location search” relation table

|===
|Location |Focus           |Threshold|Clue
| L1     | P4, L3, L4, T4 |5.2  |C8
|  *     | L4, C5, C6     |5.0  |C3
| L5     |     L6         |8.0  |C12
|===

*Location* — ID of the location being searched.

[[search-focus]]  
*Focus* — table search focus. Serves to increase the probability of discovering clues that have relations with target entities of interest to detectives.  
In the table focus, only _IDs_ of entities related to the target clue are listed.  
Specifying individual types in the table focus is not allowed.  
You can list several IDs of the same type separated by commas — this is interpreted as any of them.

The detective focus may consist of one or more IDs of *discovered* entities or any entity types.  
For example: “P1, C1, T?”. The maximum number of elements in the detective focus is determined in the scenario (usually 3).

*Threshold* — clue discovery threshold. A _real_ number that is compared with the value rolled on the D12 die.  
If the value rolled is less than the “Threshold”, it is considered that negative factors have come into play and the target clue is not discovered.  
In the scenario, the discovery threshold is set in such a way that the clues most important for the investigation have higher threshold values. The ability to specify fractional values allows clues to be ordered “finely”.

To reduce the influence of randomness, small threshold values should be specified; to exclude randomness — less than 1.

*Clue* — ID of the target clue. The discovered clue card is placed face up. To indicate the place where the clue was found, a token with the ID of the discovered clue is placed on the location.

=== Search cost, point awarding

[[search-cost]]  
*Search cost* — the resource cost of a search is the same for all locations and is determined in the scenario.  
It determines how many resources are spent per search in a location. The detective learns the cost at the beginning of the game.

[[search-points]]  
*Search points* — the base number of points that may be awarded to the detective for searching a location.  
For all locations in the scenario, the same base number of points is determined.  
Base points are awarded for discovering a clue, but only if it was _NOT_ *<<accidental-discovery, accidentally discovered>>*.  
When awarding base points, one additional point is awarded for each match of an _entity_ in the detective focus with the table focus.

== Algorithm for determining the target clue in the location search table

The goal of the algorithm is to determine the clue that best matches the detective focus and the D12 roll.

The algorithm considers only the rows of the search table relating to the selected location.  
Rows for which the clue was already discovered in previous searches are excluded from consideration.

1. For each row, the focus weights EQ, EQT, NE are calculated (see <<focus-weights-comp, calculation of focus match weights>>).
2. The table rows are ordered by descending “EQ”, descending “EQT”, ascending “NE”, descending *Threshold*.
3. Sequentially, starting from the first row, the “Threshold” is compared with the number on the D12 die.
4. If the D12 value >= “Threshold”, the scan stops. The clue is discovered and revealed.  
   The detective is told how many points were awarded.

[start=5]
[[accidental-discovery]]  
5. If no suitable row is found (the D12 value is insufficient), *“Accidental discovery”* of a clue occurs.  
   The clue with the lowest threshold among the considered (not yet discovered) clues is revealed.  
   The detective is informed that a suspicious clue was accidentally discovered.
6. The matching elements (entities + types) of the _detective focus_ with the table focus of the discovered clue are reported:
   * Matched entity IDs and types in the detective focus;
   * Types of unmatched entity IDs in the table focus.

====
For example, for detective focus “P2, T4, C?” and table focus “P1, P2, T4, L2”, the following will be output:  
“In location L1, clue C2 was found, related to P2, T4, P?, L?”.
====

[start=7]
7. If all clues of the location are discovered, the token “All clues discovered” is placed on the location. Further searches of the location are impossible.

[[focus-weights-comp]]  
=== Calculation of focus match weights

The following focus match weights between the detective focus and the table focus are calculated:

* EQ = [number of matched entity IDs in the focuses],
* EQT = [number of matched types in the focuses],
* NE = [number of *NON*-matched entities or types] in the detective's search focus and in the table focus.

First, “EQ” is calculated by matching IDs in both focuses. Then the matched IDs are excluded from both focuses.  
To calculate “EQT”, the entity types directly indicated in the detective focus (P?, L?, …) are compared with the types of the remaining IDs in the table focus. Elements matched during “EQT” calculation are excluded from both focuses.  
“NE” is calculated as the total number of remaining entities in the focuses.

When calculating “EQT”, an element of type “X?” in the detective focus is considered matched with an entity of the table focus (with an ID) if the type “X?” corresponds to the type in the entity ID.

For example, detective focus P1, P2, T? compared with table focus P2, T4, C1 gives:  
EQ = 1 (for P2), EQT = 1 (for T?), NE = 2 (for P1, C1).

== Personage interrogation

With each personage, the scenario associates certain clues.  
It is assumed that some of them were with the personage before the crime (for example, clothing, personal items, personality).  
Some they acquired during the crime — picked up in locations or received from other personages (for example, a knife, a note).  
Some clues of the personage describe their inherent traits (for example, habits, addiction to alcohol, personality, etc.). The remaining part of the clues are the personage's testimony.  
The scenario also assumes that during the crime, personages may leave clues in locations, pass or plant them to other personages when meeting them in the same location.

Some of the personage's clues may be known to detectives (for example, clothing, etc.) before the investigation.  
Another part of the clues is hidden.

Similarly to the location search procedure, to discover such clues related to a personage, a detective may *“interrogate”* any discovered personage (including a previously hidden one).  
As a result of the interrogation, another personage clue may be discovered and a relation to other clues established.

During interrogation, the detective “asks” the personage about facts known to the investigation (discovered key entities) or about the investigation's hypotheses (about any entity types) — that is, determines the *interrogation focus*.  
If the personage “knows” anything about this, a clue related to the focus and its relations are revealed.  
The interrogation mechanic is built in such a way that it is difficult for a detective to reveal a relevant target personage clue without knowledge of key entities or their types.

During the answer, the personage may *“tell a lie”* — a clue is revealed that is in fact not related to the key entities in the interrogation focus.  
The scenario regulates the number of “false” clues. The falsity of a clue can be established only through deductive analysis.  
The “Answers” section lists which clues were actually false.

Each personage is to some degree unwilling to share certain information, and the detective uses psychological pressure to obtain maximum information.  
This interaction has a random nature and is modeled with a D12 roll.

Personage interrogation costs a certain resource. Points may be awarded to the detective for the interrogation.

The scenario defines the *“Personage interrogation”* relation table, which determines the relation between personages and clues.

Interrogation procedure:

1. The detective selects the personage to interrogate, for example P1.
2. Specifies the detective focus — a set of IDs of any *discovered* entities or any types, for example L1, P?.
3. Rolls D12 to model psychological pressure on the personage.
4. Using the interrogation table, the interrogation result is determined.  
   Access to the interrogation table is provided through the Master Program.

=== “Personage interrogation” relation table

|===
|Personage |Focus             |Threshold |Clue
|P1       |P2, L3, C4, C5, T2| 3.0  |C3
|P4       |C4, T5, T6        | 5.0  |C12
| *       |P5                | 5.2  |C15
|===

*Personage* — the personage being interrogated.  
The detective, on the basis of clues known from the investigation and logical conclusions, chooses the personage to interrogate.

*Focus* — table interrogation focus (the same as <<search-focus, table search focus>>).

The detective focus is subject to a restriction:  
*Among the elements of the detective focus, there must be at least one entity ID.*

*Threshold* — threshold of resistance to the detective's psychological pressure.  
A _real_ number that is compared with the value rolled on the D12 die.  
If the value rolled is less than the “Threshold”, it is considered that the detective could not overcome the personage's unwillingness to share information, and therefore the target clue is not revealed.  
In the scenario, the discovery threshold is set in such a way that the clues most important for the investigation have higher threshold values. The ability to specify fractional values allows clues to be ordered “finely”.

To reduce the influence of randomness, small threshold values should be specified; to exclude randomness — less than 1.

*Clue* — the target personage clue that is revealed upon a successful interrogation.  
The clue may be intentionally specified incorrectly by the scenario — simulating that the personage is lying / has forgotten.

=== Interrogation cost, awarded points

*Interrogation cost* — the resource cost of interrogating any personages is the same and is determined in the scenario.  
It determines how much resource is spent per personage interrogation. The detective learns the cost at the beginning of the game.

*Interrogation points* — the base number of points that may be awarded to the detective for interrogating a personage.  
For all personages in the scenario, the same base number of points is determined.  
Base points are awarded for discovering the target clue during interrogation (false or not).  
When awarding base points (if the clue is revealed), one additional point is awarded for each match of an _entity_ in the focus.

[[interrogation-alg]]  
=== Algorithm for determining the target clue in the personage interrogation table

The algorithm for determining the target clue pursues the following goal: to determine the personage clue that best matches the detective focus, but the clues must be revealed starting with the least significant for the investigation.

The algorithm considers the rows of the interrogation table relating to the selected personage.  
Rows for which the clue was already discovered in previous interrogations are excluded from consideration.

1. For each row, the focus weights EQ, EQT, NE are calculated (see <<focus-weights-comp, calculation of focus match weights>>).
2. The row with maximum “EQ”, maximum “EQT”, minimum “NE”, and minimum *Threshold* is found.
3. If for this row “EQ = 0”, the personage does not know what the detective is asking about. The target clue is not revealed.  
   A report is made that the personage has no needed information:
   * Unmatched entity IDs and types from the detective focus.
   * Entity types of unmatched IDs in the table focus (as a hint at other relations).
====
For example, for detective focus “L3, P?, T?” and table focus “L4, T1, C2”, the following will be output:  
“Personage P1 does not know about L3, P?, but hints that they know about L?, T?, C?”.
====

[start=4]
4. If “EQ > 0”, the personage knows what the detective is asking about.  
   For the report, the following are selected:
   * Matched entity IDs and types in the detective focus.
   * Entity types of unmatched IDs in the table focus.

5. If “D12 >= Threshold”, the target clue is revealed. The discovery of the clue and relations is reported:

====
For example, for target clue C8, detective focus “L3, P?, C?” and table focus “L3, P7, P4, T2”, the following will be output:  

“Personage P1 knows about L3, P?, P?, T?, related to C8”.  
====

The detective is told how many points were awarded.
The row corresponding to the target clue is excluded from further interrogations.  

If all clues of the personage are discovered, the token “All clues discovered” is placed on the personage card. Further interrogations of the personage are impossible.

[start=6]   
6. If “D12 < Threshold”, the target clue is not revealed. Only the relations are reported:  
“Personage P1 knows about L3, P?, P?, T?, but did not want to share details”.

== Expertise

Expertise is any procedural action to establish relations between *discovered* entities that have no direct references to each other. For example: determine whether ash found in a location matches cigarettes found on a personage; conduct a ballistic expertise; crack a digital storage medium, etc.

During expertise, there are random factors that affect its result.  
This influence is modeled by a D12 roll.

Expertise costs a certain resource. Points may be awarded to the detective for expertise.

The scenario defines the *“Expertise”* relation table, which determines the relation between entities.

Expertise procedure:

1. The detective specifies the detective focus — a set of IDs of any *discovered* entities or any types, for example C1, P?.
2. Rolls a D12 die to model the influence of random factors.
3. Using the “Expertise” table, the expertise result is determined.  
   This table is available only through the Master Program.
4. Upon completion of the expertise, the “Expert report” card is filled in, indicating the expertise results.

=== “Expertise” relation table

|===
|Focus          |Threshold|Clue
| P2, L3, L5, T6|10.0 | C2
| P3, C3, C4    |7.1  | C4
|===

*Focus* — table expertise focus (the same as <<search-focus, table search focus>>).

The detective focus is subject to a restriction:  
*Among the elements of the detective focus, there must be at least two entity IDs.*

*Threshold* — threshold of the influence of random factors on expertise.  
A _real_ number that is compared with the value rolled on the D12 die.  
If the value rolled is less than the “Threshold”, it is considered that the negative influence of factors is significant, the expertise was performed poorly, and the target clue is not revealed.  
In the scenario, the influence threshold is set in such a way that the clues most important for the investigation have higher threshold values. The ability to specify fractional values allows clues to be ordered “finely”.

To reduce the influence of randomness, small threshold values should be specified; to exclude randomness — less than 1.

*Clue* — the target clue, essentially an expert report detailing the relation.  
For example: “The discovered fingerprints match”.

=== Expertise cost, awarded points

*Expertise cost* — the resource cost of any expertise is the same and is determined in the scenario.  
It determines how much resource is spent per expertise. The detective learns the cost at the beginning of the game.

*Expertise points* — the base number of points that may be awarded to the detective for expertise.  
For all expertises in the scenario, the same base number of points is determined.  
Base points are awarded to the detective for discovering a clue.  
When awarding base points (when a clue is revealed), one additional point is awarded for each match of an _entity_ in the focus.

== Algorithm for identifying a relation row in the expertise table

The algorithm for determining the relation pursues the following goal: to determine the presence of a relation and additional data for entities that best match the detective focus and the D12 roll.

Rows for which the target clue was revealed in previous expertises are excluded from consideration.

[[expertise-quality]]  
1. For each row, the focus weights EQ, EQT, NE are calculated (see <<focus-weights-comp, calculation of focus match weights>>).
2. The row with maximum “EQ”, maximum “EQT”, minimum “NE”, minimum *Threshold* is found.
3. If “EQ = 0”, no relations are identified. The target clue is not revealed.  
A report is made about the non-identification of a relation:
   * Unmatched entity IDs and types from the detective focus.
   * Entity types of unmatched IDs in the table focus (as recommendations).
====
For example: “The relation between L2, P4, C? was not identified. It is recommended to investigate L?, P?, T?”.
====

[start=4]
4. If “EQ = 1”, relations for one entity are identified. The target clue is not revealed. A report is made about relations “in general”:
   * Matched entity IDs in the detective focus.
   * Entity types in the table focus, except those matched with IDs from the detective focus.

====
For example, for detective focus “L2, L3, P?” and table focus “L2, L4, P1, T3”:  

“A relation of L2 with L?, P?, T? was identified”.
====

[start=5]
5. If “EQ > 1”, a relation is discovered.  
For the report, the following are prepared:

   * Matched entity IDs in the detective focus.
   * Entity types in the table focus, except those matched with IDs from the detective focus.

[start=6]
6. If “D12 >= Threshold”, the influence of factors is low, and the target clue is revealed.  

====
Report on the discovery of a relation and the revelation of a clue (expert report):  
   “A relation of L2, P4 with L?, T?, P? was discovered. Expert report C3”.  
   The detective is told how many points were awarded.
====

The row with the revealed clue is excluded from further expertises.  

If target clues for all rows of the expertise table are revealed, the token “Expertise unavailable” is placed. Further expertise is impossible.

[start=7]
7. If D12 < Threshold, the influence of factors is high, and the target clue is not revealed.  

====   
Report: “A relation of L2, P4 with L?, T?, P? was discovered. The expertise was difficult to perform”.

====

[[focus-stat-hint]]  
== Focus statistics

Obtaining table focus statistics is an optional mechanic; the possibility of use is regulated in the scenario.  
Obtaining statistics may be allowed to reduce game difficulty for beginners.

Obtaining focus statistics may be either free or cost a certain resource (specified in the scenario).

Statistics are calculated at the moment of request — rows for which target clues have already been revealed are not taken into account.

[[location-glue-hint]]  
=== Location statistics

Before conducting a search, the detective may request the current statistics of a location:

1. The percentage of each entity type in all table focuses of the location is calculated.
2. Statistics are output: “Possible relations of the location with P 45%, L 30%, T 25%, C 0%”.

[[personage-glue-hint]]  
=== Personage statistics

Before conducting an interrogation, the detective may request the current statistics of a personage:

1. The percentage of each entity type in all table focuses of the personage is calculated.
2. Statistics are output: “The personage may know about P 45%, L 30%, T 25%, C 0%”.

[[expertise-glue-hint]]  
=== Expertise statistics

Before conducting expertise, the detective may request the current statistics of expertise:

1. The percentage of each entity type in all table focuses of expertise is calculated.
2. Statistics are output: “Expertise may determine relations between P 45%, L 30%, T 25%, C 0%”.

== Direct entity relations

A direct relation between any entities is possible through a mention in a clue, as well as in the “Expertise” table.  
Relations are also specified according to the table:

|===
| |Location |Time |Clue |Personage

|*Location* |Ways between locations| Clue |Discovery of a clue in a location| Clue
|*Time* | * |Adjacent ticks|Clue|Clue
|*Clue* | *  | *  |Clue| Discovery of a clue on a personage
|*Personage* | * | *  | *  |Clue
|===

== Answers

The “Answers” section is described in the scenario and revealed at the end of the game.  
The section consists of several subsections, each of which reveals certain aspects of the crime.  
Some answers are contained in the descriptions of other entities, for example, which locations and passages are hidden, the falsity of clues, event logs, the importance of clues for the investigation, etc.

=== Crime description

The course of the crime, the locations involved, personages, and clues are briefly described in words.

=== Recommended investigation strategy

The recommended sequence of search, interrogation, expertise. Which clues to use, which to expect.
